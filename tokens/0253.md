# BRC-253: Countersigned Tokens and Items

Brandon Cryderman / HandCash (brandongcryderman@gmail.com)

**Status:** Draft (proposed number, not a BSVA assignment)

## Abstract

A countersigned asset is a [BRC-162](./0162.md) token whose issuer must accept every transfer before it exists. Every asset output has a locking script that needs two signatures: the holder's and the issuer's. The issuer's signature is the acceptance. A transfer the issuer refused cannot be valid Bitcoin, so it can never be broadcast, mined or credited.

An asset is either a fungible **token** or a non-fungible **item**. An item is a deploy with a supply of exactly one, so tokens and items share one script, one acceptance flow and one resolution rule, and are filed under their own profiles, `csft` and `csnft`. Items may belong to a collection that shares their issuer.

The sender asks the issuer for acceptance peer to peer. The request goes to the issuer's identity key over a [BRC-246](../peer-to-peer/0246.md) data channel, or through a [BRC-33](../peer-to-peer/0033.md) message box when no channel is open. The issuer publishes no endpoint. The recipient checks a transfer offline from one [BRC-30](../transactions/0030.md) Extended Format transaction. It walks no lineage and needs no overlay. Once those checks pass, the transfer is final against the sender.

Every output also names a **release** key set, held by custodians who sign nothing while the issuer is alive. When the issuer retires, or its on-chain beacon lapses, the custodians publish the release private keys in one transaction. From then on anyone can countersign, and the asset is an owner-only token. A freeze holds while the issuer is alive, and holders are never locked out after it is gone.

Countersigning is for assets whose value comes from their issuer, such as handles, stablecoins and tickets. Such an asset is worth little without its issuer, so this document does not keep it under control after the issuer is gone. Art, collectables and open tokens need no third party, and stay ordinary [BRC-162](./0162.md) tokens and [BRC-150](./0150.md) items.

## Motivation

An issuer of a regulated asset, such as a stablecoin, must be able to refuse a transfer: from a frozen holder, to an unregistered recipient, or over a limit. Today that is done by an overlay that admits each transfer ([BRC-207](../wallet/0207.md)), with attestations so that a payee can accept offline ([BRC-175](../payments/0175.md)). That design has two costs:

- **Latency.** A wallet needs a current overlay answer before it spends or accepts. Accepting before that answer needs attestations, a walk to attested outputs, and settlement later.
- **Unclear outcomes.** A payment can be credited and later reversed when settlement fails, or held without being credited. An attestation shows the output was admitted, not that it is still unspent. A payer's inputs stay reserved after a decline. Trust modes, complete-set rules and eviction all decide what a balance means.

Both costs come from the same place. Acceptance is a statement about a transaction, made outside it, so a transaction can exist without the statement, and parties must reason about the gap.

This document closes the gap. The issuer's acceptance is a signature inside the transaction, and the locking script makes it necessary. There are three outcomes and each is final: the issuer signed, the issuer refused, or the request was cancelled.

**Scope.** An asset that needs a third party to agree to every transfer cannot be paid with the sender offline, and has no value that outlives that party. One party may still be offline: the recipient. The sender and the issuer must be online for the transfer; the delivery waits in the recipient's message box, and the recipient resolves it whenever it next connects, with no network needed for the check itself. After a release (§9) the issuer is no longer needed; the recipient may still be offline, but credits the transfer only once it is mined. This document is therefore meant only for assets whose value comes from their issuer: a handle registry, a stablecoin, a ticket. It does not try to keep issuer control after the issuer is gone. It only guarantees that holders can move what they hold.

## Terms

- **Issuer key set.** `m` and `n` with `1 ≤ m ≤ n ≤ 5`, and `n` compressed secp256k1 public keys, fixed at deploy. Any `m` of them accept a transfer. These keys are online, and sign automatically under the issuer's policy.
- **Release key set.** `k` and `j` with `1 ≤ k ≤ j ≤ 5`, and `j` compressed public keys, fixed at deploy. No release key may also be an issuer key. Its custodians sign nothing while the issuer is alive. Keys the issuer does not hold SHOULD meet `k` on their own, so that a release survives the issuer vanishing or losing its own keys. A set only the issuer can meet freezes the asset for good if the issuer stops without releasing it. A wallet MUST NOT choose such a set by default, and MUST tell the issuer this consequence before deploying one.
- **Asset.** A token or an item deployed under this document. Its **asset id** is the deploy outpoint, as BRC-162 defines the token id. Where this document says "token" about a rule, the rule applies to every asset.
- **Item.** An asset whose deploy has `kind` `"item"` and a fixed supply of 1.
- **Collection.** An asset that items name with `col`. Its members share its key sets and its beacon.
- **Countersign clause.** The locking script below, written after the BRC-162 prefix.
- **Countersigned output.** An output with the asset id in its BRC-162 prefix, and the countersign clause naming the deploy's issuer and release key sets.
- **Acceptor.** The issuer key set, which accepts transfers while the asset is live (§9). A released asset has no acceptor.
- **Beacon.** A 1-satoshi output that the issuer re-spends to show on chain that it is alive (§9).
- **Live, ended, released.** The phases of an asset, read from its beacon chain (§9).
- **Resolved.** A received output that passed the checks in [§11](#11-resolution). A resolved output counts in the balance and may be spent.

## Specification

### 1. Deploy

The deploy is a BRC-162 deploy at output 0, with fixed supply or authority. Every token output it creates, value or authority, is a countersigned output, and the issuer is its first holder, under its own holder key (§2). The token id is the deploy outpoint.

Output 1 is the first beacon, of 1 satoshi, locked by the beacon script of §9.

Its payload MUST be a DAG-CBOR map containing:

- `req` with exactly the entry `{"countersign": 1}`;
- `sil`, the silence period in seconds, an unsigned integer of at least 604800 (7 days).

It MAY contain:

- BRC-162's `sym`, `dec` and `icon`;
- `kind`, `"token"` or `"item"`, as text; absent means `"token"`;
- `hdl`, the issuer's [BRC-169](../peer-to-peer/0169.md) handle, as text.

The payload MUST NOT contain a URL. Acceptors are reached by identity key, never by an address in the token.

Both key sets are read from the deploy output's clause. A wallet obtains the deploy body once per asset from any source, and keeps it only when the body hashes to the asset id's txid.

**Items.** An item deploy has `kind` `"item"`, a fixed supply of exactly 1, no authority, and no `dec`. It MAY add:

- `name`, as text;
- `ctype`, the media type of its content. The content is output 2 of the deploy, `OP_FALSE OP_RETURN <content>`. Because the deploy body hashes to the asset id, the content is fixed by the id and needs no indexer.
- `col`, the asset id of its collection, as text.

**Collections.** A collection is any asset of this document with a beacon, typically an item that stands for the set. A member names it with `col`, and its deploy:

- carries the same issuer and release key sets as the collection's, and MUST NOT contain `sil`;
- spends the collection's current beacon at input 0, and writes the next beacon at output 1.

The member deploy is therefore signed by `m` issuer keys, and the collection's beacon chain lists every member in order. A wallet checks membership from the member's deploy in Extended Format: input 0 MUST pass script validation against the claimed beacon script with the collection's issuer set. A `col` claim that fails this check, or whose key sets differ from the collection's, is no membership. Members follow the collection's beacon and release (§9).

Copying an issuer's key sets into a deploy gives a forger nothing to hand out: its outputs move only with an acceptor's countersignature, an acceptor countersigns only outputs it accepted, and a recipient never resolves a deploy (§11).

### 2. Countersign clause

```
OP_DUP OP_HASH160 <holder pubkey hash, 20 bytes> OP_EQUALVERIFY OP_CHECKSIGVERIFY
OP_IF
  OP_m <issuer key 1> … <issuer key n> OP_n OP_CHECKMULTISIG
OP_ELSE
  OP_k <release key 1> … <release key j> OP_j OP_CHECKMULTISIG
OP_ENDIF
```

`OP_m`, `OP_n`, `OP_k` and `OP_j` are `OP_1` to `OP_5`. Keys are 33-byte compressed, in the order the deploy lists them. The full locking script is:

```
<token id> <amount> OP_2DROP [<payload> OP_DROP] <countersign clause>
```

The unlocking script is:

```
OP_0 <countersignature 1> … <countersignature m or k> <branch> <holder signature> <holder pubkey>
```

`branch` is `OP_1` for the issuer branch and `OP_0` for the release branch. Countersignatures are in the order of their keys. The holder's signature MUST use sighash `ALL | ANYONECANPAY | FORKID` (`0xC1`). Every countersignature MUST use `ALL | FORKID` (`0x41`).

The holder signs only its own input and every output, so the acceptor can add its fee input without changing what the holder approved. The acceptor then signs everything, so no input or output can change after acceptance.

**Holder keys.** The holder pubkey hash is never an identity key's. It is the hash of a fresh [BRC-42](../key-derivation/0042.md) child: [BRC-29](../payments/0029.md)'s derivation, unchanged, with protocol `[2, "3241645161d8"]`, key ID `"<derivationPrefix> <derivationSuffix>"` and the holder's identity key as counterparty, its own for an output the sender keeps. The sender draws a fresh prefix per transfer and a fresh suffix per output; a deploy output is derived the same way. The holder reaches the key through BRC-100 `getPublicKey` and `createSignature` with the sender's identity key as counterparty, which no root-key or identity-address lock allows: a conforming wallet never signs with its root key. No two outputs share a holder pubkey hash, so a chain observer cannot link one holder's outputs to each other or to its identity.

Each recipient entry carries `"keyScheme": "brc29"`. An entry without it was written by an earlier version of this document, whose holder keys used protocol `[2, "p color <assetTag> <prefixTag> <suffixTag>"]` with key ID `color` (asset tag `issue` for a deploy output); an acceptor checks its linkage under that protocol, and a holder tries that derivation for an output whose BRC-29 key does not match. New outputs MUST use BRC-29.

Script timelocks are not available for the release branch: BSV restored `OP_CHECKLOCKTIMEVERIFY` and `OP_CHECKSEQUENCEVERIFY` to no-ops at Genesis. When the release keys may be used is therefore read from the beacon chain (§9), not a script condition.

### 3. Closure

Every token output of a countersigned token MUST be a countersigned output with the deploy's key sets. An acceptor MUST NOT countersign a transaction that writes the token's id to any other script. A wallet MUST treat an output of the token without the clause, or with different key sets, as not a holding of the token.

Since every spend needs an acceptor and acceptors countersign only transactions that keep the clause, every holding traces through accepted transactions back to the deploy. Nobody has to walk that history.

### 4. Building a transfer

A sender builds one transaction:

1. Inputs are resolved countersigned outputs of one token. It has no other inputs. The acceptor adds the only other one.
2. Outputs are countersigned outputs: one per recipient, locked to the pubkey hash of the recipient's holder key (§2) for fresh remittance strings, and change, when needed, locked to a holder key of the sender's own. If the acceptor's current terms (§8) ask for a fee in tokens, it adds that output too. Amounts sum exactly to the inputs.
3. Each output carries 1 satoshi. The sender does not fund the transaction.
4. The user approves the token, the amount and each recipient. A satoshi payment grant, an auto-pay limit or a generic basket grant MUST NOT approve it.
5. The holder signs every input (sighash `0xC1`). Each unlocking script holds only `<holder signature> <holder pubkey>`.

The sender serializes this transaction in Extended Format. Its **request id** is the lowercase hex SHA-256 of those bytes.

### 5. Requesting acceptance

The sender seals a request to the acceptor per BRC-78:

```json
{
  "v": 1,
  "t": "request",
  "token": "<token id, txid_vout>",
  "ef": "<Extended Format transaction, base64>",
  "recipients": [{
    "vout": 0,
    "identityKey": "<66 hex>",
    "derivationPrefix": "<remittance string>",
    "derivationSuffix": "<remittance string>",
    "keyScheme": "brc29",
    "encryptedLinkage": "<base64>",
    "encryptedLinkageProof": "<base64>"
  }]
}
```

`recipients` names the identity behind each output, so the acceptor can apply its rules about who may hold the token, and proves it. `encryptedLinkage` and `encryptedLinkageProof` are what BRC-100 `revealSpecificKeyLinkage` returns for that output's BRC-29 protocol and key ID, with the holder's identity key as counterparty and the acceptor key the request is sealed to as verifier: the BRC-69 specific linkage, encrypted to that acceptor per BRC-72. A request sent to several acceptor keys, or to the cosigners of a shared set, carries an entry encrypted to each one. The acceptor decrypts the linkage and checks that `identityKey + linkage·G` hashes to the output's holder pubkey hash. That equation is the proof: whoever spends the output needs the named identity's private key, whatever linkage the sender chose. It reveals that one output's owner to the acceptor and nothing more.

A sender-funded trade (§6) adds `"funding": "sender"`. Its plain outputs need no entry.

The sender MUST list every output, its change included, with its own identity key for change. An entry with only `vout` and `identityKey` names an output locked to that identity key's own pubkey hash, as the previous revision of this document wrote them; an acceptor MAY accept it.

The sender sends it on an open BRC-246 data channel to any device of an acceptor key. Otherwise it sends it through BRC-33 `sendMessage` to that key's message box, in box `countersign`. It finds that box by the issuer's handle (`hdl`) per BRC-169, or by a box it already knows for that key. A sender MAY send the same request to several acceptor keys, because a request is accepted at most once (§6).

Requests go to the issuer only while the asset is live. A released asset needs no request: the holder countersigns with the disclosed release keys itself (§9).

While a request is outstanding, the sender MUST NOT spend its inputs in a different transaction. It MAY send the same request again. Repeating a request is safe.

### 6. Acceptor processing

The acceptor keeps the set of countersigned outputs accepted and not yet seen spent. The outputs of its own deploys enter that set when the deploy is broadcast. For a request it:

1. checks that every input is in that set and is not reserved by another request;
2. checks every holder signature, the clause and key set of every output, and that amounts sum exactly;
3. checks each entry's linkage (§5), refusing with `recipient` an output whose linkage does not open or does not hash to its holder pubkey hash, and an output that is neither listed nor locked to an input's holder pubkey hash. It applies its own policy to the identities behind the inputs and the listed outputs: frozen holders, registered recipients, sanctions, limits. It knows who holds each input because it recorded the identity behind every output it accepted;
4. checks its fee terms;
5. in one durable step: marks the inputs spent by this request, adds one input of its own whose value covers the network fee and any carrier-satoshi deficit, countersigns every token input on its own branch, signs its own input, and records the signed transaction under the request id;
6. replies, delivers (§10) and broadcasts the transaction. Replying does not wait for broadcast.

**Accept once.** An acceptor MUST NOT countersign two transactions spending the same output, except as a sender-funded trade allows (below). A repeated request returns the transaction already recorded. The fee input adds no change output, because the holder already signed the outputs. The acceptor keeps inputs of a few fixed sizes for this.

Because the only non-token input belongs to the acceptor, the sender has nothing left to double spend once a transfer is accepted.

**Replacement.** An accepted transaction may never be mined: the network refuses it, for example for too low a fee, or one of the acceptor's own inputs is spent elsewhere. Its fee cannot be raised: every sequence is final, and the acceptor owns no output to spend in a child. The acceptor MAY then countersign a replacement, and only when all of these hold:

1. the network refused the original, or a transaction other than the original that spends one of the original's non-token inputs is mined;
2. no output of the original is an input of another transaction the acceptor accepted;
3. the replacement spends the same token inputs with the same holder signatures, writes byte-identical outputs in the same order, and differs only in the acceptor's own input.

The holder's `0xC1` signature covers only its own input and the outputs, so it stays valid and the holder is not asked again. The replacement and the original spend the same token inputs, so at most one is mined, and both pay the same holders the same amounts. The acceptor records the replacement under the original request id, answers a repeated request with it, and delivers it (§10). It carries the same spend, so it is not a second acceptance.

When the acceptor accepts a spend of an output whose transaction is not yet mined, its input SHOULD pay the fee of both, so the new transaction carries its parent.

**Shared issuer sets.** An issuer set of `m` of `n` keys with `n ≥ 2` belongs to `n` **cosigners**, each holding one key and keeping its own accept-once record. `2m` MUST be greater than `n`, so any two quorums share a cosigner, and that cosigner refuses the second of two spends of one output. An issuing wallet refuses a set that breaks this. Such a transfer differs from §4 to §6 in five ways.

1. The sender funds the transaction. After the token inputs it adds inputs of its own that are not countersigned outputs, covering the fee, and it signs them `SIGHASH_ALL|FORKID`. Every cosigner then signs the same bytes. All sequences are final, so `nLockTime` is free: the sender sets a fresh value so that each attempt has a new request id.
2. The sender sends the request to every cosigner. It MAY attach `beef`, the request's unproven ancestors as BRC-95 Atomic BEEF.
3. A cosigner accepts an input it did not record when the transaction that wrote it spent every countersigned input on the issuer branch with a valid unlocking script and resolves (§11). The deploy output is accepted by definition. In one durable step it reserves the token inputs under the request id and countersigns each of them. It adds no input and replies `cosigned` (§7).
4. A cosigner's refusal of a request is final: it never signs that request id later.
5. The sender orders `m` countersignatures by the issuer set's key order, as `OP_CHECKMULTISIG` requires, checks each input, and broadcasts. If fewer than `m` cosigners can still sign, the request is refused. The sender then sends `cancel` with more than `n − m` signed refusals of that request to each cosigner that signed it. Such a cosigner frees its reservation on that proof, because without it its signature may already belong to a quorum.

A cosigner also frees its reservation once a transaction other than the request that spends one of the request's non-token inputs is mined. The request can then never be mined, so the signature cannot complete a valid spend. This needs no other cosigner: a sender that can gather neither `m` countersignatures nor the refusals, because cosigners are offline, frees its token inputs by spending one of its own fee inputs elsewhere and showing that transaction once it is mined.

A sender that funds its own transfer can therefore void a countersigned transfer by double spending a fee input. It gets back only the token inputs it already held, but a recipient that had credited the transfer would lose it. A recipient therefore credits a transfer of a shared-set asset only once it is mined, as after a release (§9).

The other cosigners join by consent. The issuing wallet sends each of them `{ "v": 1, "t": "join", "token": "…", "deploy": "<deploy transaction, hex>" }`. A cosigner countersigns nothing for the asset until its user joins.

**Sender-funded trades.** A trade pays satoshis, or another asset, against countersigned inputs in one transaction. Its request carries `"funding": "sender"` and differs from §4 to §6 in six ways. Any acceptor applies it, whether the issuer set is one key or shared.

1. The sender funds the transaction as for a shared set: its plain inputs follow the countersigned ones and are signed `SIGHASH_ALL|FORKID`. The acceptor adds no input and replies `cosigned` (§7). The sender broadcasts.
2. Outputs may be plain, beside the countersigned outputs. Countersigned outputs still carry 1 satoshi, conserve each asset and name their recipients (§5, §6 steps 2 and 3).
3. A holder MAY sign its input `SIGHASH_SINGLE|ANYONECANPAY|FORKID` (`0xC3`) instead of `0xC1`. That signature covers its own input and the output at the same index only, so a holder can sign a sale before any buyer exists. The bound output MUST be plain: it is the holder's payment. An acceptor refuses a `0xC3` input whose output at that index is missing or countersigned, because a holder that signed it could otherwise lose the asset to whoever completed the transaction.
4. The acceptor reserves the countersigned inputs under the request id, as in step 3 of a shared set. An earlier signed request spending one of them does not stop the new one when that earlier input's holder signed `0xC3`, or when the two share a plain input. Both spend the same output, so at most one is mined, and whichever is mined pays what its holders signed for. The holder of a sale is paid either way; a buyer whose transaction is not mined keeps its coins.
5. Any other earlier sender-funded request stops the new one until a transaction that spends one of its plain inputs is mined, as for a shared set. A transaction the acceptor funded never stops being final.
6. A recipient credits the trade only once it is mined, as for a shared set: the sender can void it by spending a plain input elsewhere.

A **market listing** is a sale signed ahead. The holder first moves the listed amount to an output of its own with a transfer, then signs that output `0xC3` against its price. A buyer later appends its own countersigned output, change and coins, and sends the trade as its own request, with a linkage entry (§5) for its output. The acceptor checks the seller's signature and the buyer's linkage, and countersigns. A listing is cancelled by any other spend of that output. A buyer cannot ask for countersigned assets in the same way, because the acceptor must see the buyer's receive output proven before the seller can be paid for it.

### 7. Decisions

A reply is sealed to the sender per BRC-78 and returned on the channel or in the sender's box `countersign`.

```json
{ "v": 1, "t": "accepted", "token": "…", "request": "<request id>", "ef": "<signed Extended Format transaction, base64>" }
{ "v": 1, "t": "refused",  "token": "…", "request": "<request id>", "reason": "<code>", "terms": { "…": "§8, when reason is fee" }, "signature": "…" }
{ "v": 1, "t": "cancelled", "token": "…", "request": "<request id>", "signature": "…" }
```

`accepted` needs no further signature. The transaction is the proof.

A cosigner of a shared issuer set (§6) replies `{ "v": 1, "t": "cosigned", "token": "…", "request": "<request id>", "signatures": ["<countersignature per token input, checksig hex>"] }`. Each signature is its own proof: the sender verifies it against the request before keeping it. A `cancel` to a cosigner carries `"refusals": { "<cosigner key>": { "t": "refused" | "cancelled", "reason": "…", "signature": "…" } }`.

An `accepted` reply MAY also carry `beef`: the accepted transaction as [BRC-95](../transactions/0095.md) Atomic BEEF, base64. Resolution never needs it (§11). It lets a wallet whose storage only admits BEEF file the outputs without fetching the acceptor's fee input from a chain source.

`refused` and `cancelled` are signed by an acceptor key with the BRC-42 child for protocol `[2, "countersign decision"]`, key ID `1`, counterparty `anyone`. The signature is DER-encoded ECDSA, with low-S and RFC 6979 nonces, over SHA-256 of the UTF-8 preimage `countersign\nv1\n{t}\n{token}\n{request}\n{reason}`, where `reason` is empty for `cancelled`. Every signed statement in this document uses this construction. Its preimage always starts `countersign\nv1\n{t}\n{token}`.

Reason codes: `unknown_input`, `spent`, `invalid`, `frozen`, `recipient`, `limit`, `fee`, `policy`, `not_acceptor`. Wallets SHOULD show the reason to the user. `not_acceptor` comes from an issuer asked about an asset that has ended (§9).

After `refused` or `cancelled`, the inputs are the sender's to use again. That request id is never accepted.

**Cancel.** A sender with an outstanding request MAY send `{ "v": 1, "t": "cancel", "token": "…", "request": "…" }`. The acceptor answers with `accepted` if it already accepted, or with `cancelled`. Either answer ends the request.

A sender with no answer waits. It loses nothing, because without an answer no transaction exists.

### 8. Terms

An acceptor that charges a fee in tokens publishes signed terms over `countersign\nv1\nterms\n{token}\n{fee}\n{feeScript}\n{validUntil}`:

```json
{ "v": 1, "t": "terms", "token": "…", "fee": "<units, decimal string>", "feeScript": "<countersigned locking script, hex>", "validUntil": 0, "signature": "…" }
```

`validUntil` is in Unix seconds. A sender caches the terms and applies them until they expire. A `fee` refusal carries the current terms, so a retry succeeds. An acceptor that charges nothing publishes none, and a request needs no fee output.

### 9. Release

**Beacon.** The beacon script is:

```
OP_IF
  OP_m <issuer key 1> … <issuer key n> OP_n OP_CHECKMULTISIG
OP_ELSE
  OP_k <release key 1> … <release key j> OP_j OP_CHECKMULTISIG
OP_ENDIF
```

It is unlocked by `OP_0 <signatures> OP_1` on the issuer branch, or `OP_0 <signatures> OP_0` on the release branch, each signature with sighash `ALL | FORKID`.

The issuer keeps exactly one beacon per asset with a beacon. The first is output 1 of the deploy. To refresh it, the issuer spends the current beacon on the issuer branch into output 0 of a new transaction, with the same script. A collection member's deploy also refreshes it, at output 1 (§1). A transaction that spends a beacon has at most one output with the beacon script, and that output is the next beacon. Anyone can follow the beacon chain from the deploy, spend by spend, with any chain source. The issuer MUST get a refresh mined at least once every `sil / 2` seconds.

With a shared issuer set any cosigner may propose the refresh. The proposer funds it, sends `{ "v": 1, "t": "beacon-sign", "token": "…", "ef": "<refresh, input 0 unsigned, Extended Format base64>" }`, and assembles `m` replies `{ "v": 1, "t": "beacon-signed", "token": "…", "proposal": "<SHA-256 of those bytes, hex>", "signature": "…" }`. A cosigner signs any proposal whose input 0 spends a beacon of the asset and whose only output is the next beacon. Two refreshes of one beacon cannot both be mined, so signing both is harmless. Cosigners SHOULD take turns by key order to avoid duplicate proposals. A cosigner reads the lapse from the beacon chain rather than from its own last refresh. One cosigner cannot retire a shared asset: it withdraws and stops signing. The asset lapses once too few cosigners remain to refresh the beacon.

For a collection, everything below is about the collection's asset id and applies to every member.

**Phases.** Every wallet reads an asset's phase from its beacon chain alone:

- **Live** while neither of the conditions below holds. The issuer accepts transfers (§6).
- **Ended** once either holds:
  - **Retired.** The issuer spent the beacon on the issuer branch into a transaction whose output 0 is the beacon script, the **final beacon**, and that has an output `OP_FALSE OP_RETURN "countersign retire" <asset id>`.
  - **Lapsed.** Two consecutive beacons were mined more than `sil` seconds apart by block time, or the latest beacon is mined and unspent and the chain tip's block time is more than `sil` seconds after its block's. A refresh still waiting in the mempool does not count. A lapse is permanent: a later refresh does not make the asset live again.
- **Released** once `k` release keys spent the latest beacon on the release branch into a transaction that writes no next beacon and has an output `OP_FALSE OP_RETURN "countersign release" <asset id> <private key 1> … <private key k>`. Each private key is a 32-byte big-endian scalar, and they belong to `k` distinct keys of the deploy's release set. A wallet checks them against that set and ignores a disclosure that fails.

The issuer MUST NOT countersign once the asset has ended, answers `not_acceptor`, and MUST NOT spend its beacon again.

**Releasing.** Custodians MUST NOT release a live asset. Once it has ended they SHOULD release it promptly, because transfers wait until they do. An issuer that holds `k` release keys itself MAY release without retiring first; the release is then also its retirement.

**After release.** The release branch now needs only keys everyone has, so a released asset moves with its holder's signature alone: it is an owner-only token. The holder builds the transfer per §4, adds and signs a fee input of its own, countersigns each asset input with the disclosed keys, and broadcasts. It sends no request. Closure (§3) and resolution (§11) still apply, so a release adds no supply and no new kind of output.

Accept-once ends with the release, because the holder can now countersign two spends of one output. A recipient therefore credits a transfer of a released asset only once it is mined.

An input unlocked on the release branch does not show a release. `k` custodians can countersign on that branch while the asset is live without publishing anything, which would move a frozen holder's output past the issuer and outside accept-once. A wallet therefore resolves a release-branch input only once it has seen the asset's release transaction mined (§11). An issuer-branch input is final, unless the wallet already knows the asset is released.

A wallet whose request the issuer leaves unanswered, or that receives a release-branch input, SHOULD follow the beacon chain to learn the phase.

### 10. Delivery

The acceptor SHOULD deliver the accepted transaction to each recipient. The sender SHOULD deliver it too. Each delivery is sealed to the recipient per BRC-78 and sent on a BRC-246 channel or through BRC-33 to the recipient's box `countersign`, the same box that carries requests and decisions:

```json
{ "v": 1, "t": "transfer", "token": "…", "ef": "<signed Extended Format transaction, base64>", "senderIdentityKey": "<66 hex>", "outputs": [{ "vout": 0, "derivationPrefix": "…", "derivationSuffix": "…" }], "beef": "<optional Atomic BEEF, base64>", "deploy": "<optional deploy transaction, hex>" }
```

`senderIdentityKey` and `outputs` are the remittance of the recipient's own outputs, and of no other: the recipient derives each holder key from them (§11). An acceptor delivering for the sender copies them from the request's entries for that recipient, with the request's sender as `senderIdentityKey`. `beef` is as in §7. `deploy` lets a recipient that has never seen the asset read its key sets; the recipient MUST check that it hashes to the asset id's txid before using it. A recipient acknowledges a delivery only after it filed the outputs or found the transfer invalid.

A repeat of a transfer already received is the same transfer. Acceptor delivery saves the sender's leg: the recipient can hold the transfer before the sender has read the acceptor's reply.

### 11. Resolution

A recipient resolves a transfer offline:

1. It holds the deploy body for each asset id the transaction carries, and reads both key sets from it.
2. It parses the Extended Format transaction. For every input whose claimed spent script carries an asset id, that script MUST be a countersigned output with that deploy's key sets, and the input MUST pass script validation against the claimed script and satoshis.
3. Every output carrying an asset id MUST be a countersigned output with that deploy's key sets. Per asset id, amounts MUST satisfy BRC-162's transition rules, read from the claimed spent scripts: a transfer sums exactly, and only a spend of the asset's authority may mint. An item's outputs MUST total exactly 1.
4. Every asset id on an output MUST also be on an input. A recipient MUST NOT resolve a deploy, or any transaction that writes an asset without spending it.
5. For each row of the delivery's `outputs`, it derives its holder private key with `senderIdentityKey` as counterparty, and keeps the outputs whose holder pubkey hash matches. It also keeps outputs locked to its own identity key's pubkey hash, which is how a sender following the previous revision pays.
6. For every input unlocked on the release branch (`branch` is `OP_0`), the recipient MUST hold the asset's release transaction (§9), mined, with its disclosed keys checked against the deploy's release set. It follows the beacon chain to find it, and refuses the transfer if there is none. A release transaction that is not mined does not count, because custodians could hand it to one recipient without publishing it.
7. For every input unlocked on the release branch, the recipient also resolves the transaction that wrote the spent output by steps 1 to 6, and so on back until every path reaches a transaction whose inputs of the asset are all unlocked on the issuer branch, or the deploy. After a release, holders countersign for themselves and no acceptor checks amounts, so without this step anyone could write the clause onto a new output with any amount and spend it on the release branch. The sender supplies those transactions in the delivery's `beef` (§10).

One transaction MAY carry several assets of one issuer, such as an item and the tokens paid for it. Each input is countersigned on its own, and step 3 applies per asset id.

Each countersignature commits to the spent output's script and satoshis, so a false claim about either fails step 2. Acceptors sign only outputs they have accepted, so step 2 also shows the inputs are genuine. The recipient needs no parent bodies, no lineage and no overlay answer: an issuer-branch input validates from the transaction alone. A release-branch input also needs the mined release of step 6, and its history back to the last issuer-branch transfer (step 7).

The recipient MUST NOT credit an output that fails a step. It records the outputs that pass, then acknowledges the message. A resolved output of a live asset is final against the sender. The transaction needs no confirmation and no settlement step. A released asset is the exception, as §9 describes. Getting the transaction mined is the acceptor's duty: it rebroadcasts, and replaces it when it cannot be mined (§6). A replacement pays the same holders the same amounts under a new txid.

A recipient that holds an accepted transfer and receives a delivery whose transaction spends the same token inputs, with the same holder signatures and byte-identical outputs, records it as a replacement of the one it holds. The holding is the same; only its outpoints change, to whichever of the two is mined. A delivery that spends the same inputs into different outputs is not a replacement: it is the evidence of double acceptance described under Security considerations. A wallet SHOULD still broadcast the transaction it received. Broadcasting is idempotent.

### 12. Filing

This document reserves two filing names, one per kind: `csft` (countersigned fungible token) for resolved outputs of tokens and `csnft` (countersigned non-fungible token) for resolved outputs of items. After the BRC-46 normalization of trim and lowercase, conforming use MUST be exactly these. They sit beside the BRC-251 filing name `p2pft` and the BRC-165 basket `1sat` and never share them, because no holder of those can spend these outputs. A released asset (§9) stays under its countersigned filing name: its lock still carries the clause.

- Tag `<filing>:<assetId>` MUST be present, with the asset id in `txid_vout` form. The issuer's own deploy output is filed before its txid exists, so it MAY carry `<filing>:deploy` instead; its asset id is then its own outpoint, and a wallet adds the `<filing>:<assetId>` tag when it lists the row to an application.
- Tag `col:<collectionId>` MUST be present on a member whose membership checked out (§1), and absent otherwise.
- Tag `csft:auth` MAY mark an authority output, which never counts toward a balance.
- Each output carries a [BRC-164](../wallet/0164.md) `id:<key>` tag, written by the holding wallet.

When present, `customInstructions` MUST be a UTF-8 JSON object serialized as a string, with `id`, `kind`, `amt`, and `sym`, `dec`, `name`, `col` when known. An output held under a derived key also carries its remittance, `derivationPrefix`, `derivationSuffix` and `senderIdentityKey`, which is the only way back to its key: a wallet MUST keep it and include it in any history backup. Unknown members are preserved. The script is authoritative: a balance is the sum of resolved outputs' script amounts per asset id, and an item is held when its one output is resolved.

### 13. Application access

`csft` and `csnft` are [BRC-99](../wallet/0099.md) permission schemes. Each applies the [BRC-165](./0165.md) model unchanged, as BRC-251 does for `p2pft`: storage under the filing name, view through a scoped P basket, spend through a per-action label. Each is granted on its own; neither implies the other, or `p2pft` or `1sat`.

| Capability | Wire | Grants |
|---|---|---|
| View all tokens | `listOutputs` basket `p csft all` | Every resolved token output |
| View all items | `listOutputs` basket `p csnft all` | Every resolved item output |
| View by asset | `listOutputs` basket `p csft asset` or `p csnft asset` with `<filing>:<assetId>` tags | Outputs of the named assets |
| View by collection | `listOutputs` basket `p csnft collection` with `col:<collectionId>` tags | Outputs of members of the named collections |
| Look up a row | `listOutputs` basket `p csft id` or `p csnft id` with `id:<key>` tags | The resolved outputs with those keys |
| Send | `createAction` label `p csft input id <key>` or `p csnft input id <key>` | Per-action approval to spend that output under §4 |

- For `csft` the scope is exactly one of `all`, `asset` or `id`; for `csnft`, one of `all`, `asset`, `collection` or `id`. A wallet MUST refuse any other scope, and MUST refuse `asset`, `collection` or `id` without at least one matching tag.
- A spend label MUST resolve to exactly one resolved output of that filing that is an input of the same action. The wallet then runs §4 and §5 itself (or §9 for a released asset). An application never receives the holder's signature, and never addresses the acceptor.
- A wallet MUST refuse an application request that names either filing name directly.
- Payment, auto-pay and other schemes' grants MUST NOT cover these views or sends. A `p p2pft …` or `p 1sat …` request MUST NOT return a countersigned output.
- Access never changes a locking script or custody, and revoking it moves nothing. View does not authorize a send.

### 14. Basket Namespace

Per [BRC-123](../wallet/0123.md), two registrations:

1. **Scheme IDs:** `csft` (tokens) and `csnft` (items)
2. **Category:** 1 — Asset
3. **Basket name format:** `p csft <scope>` with `<scope>` `all`, `asset` or `id`; `p csnft <scope>` with `<scope>` `all`, `asset`, `collection` or `id`. Filter values ride in tags, never in the basket name. The spend label is the [BRC-111](../wallet/0111.md) form `p <scheme> input id <key>`.
4. **Wallet capability:** hold resolved outputs under the filing names as specified in §12; route `p csft …` and `p csnft …` baskets and labels to this document's module; refuse an application request that names either filing directly.
5. **Permission semantics:** see §13.

## Security considerations

- **Trust.** Holders trust the issuer to accept each output once and to stay available, and they trust the custodians not to release a live asset. That is the trust a regulated asset already asks for, made explicit, with both groups' keys visible in every output. Neither group can move a holder's tokens, because every spend also needs the holder's signature.
- **Double acceptance is provable.** Two valid transactions spending one countersigned output into different outputs, both countersigned on the issuer branch, prove that the issuer broke accept-once. Anyone holding both can show it, unless they are sender-funded trades that §6 lets conflict: a `0xC3` sale, or two requests sharing a plain input. At most one of those is mined, so they cost nobody anything. A replacement (§6) writes byte-identical outputs, so it proves nothing against the issuer.
- **Stuck transfers.** An accepted transaction whose fee is too low cannot be bumped, because the acceptor owns no output in it. The replacement of §6 is the only remedy, and it is limited to transactions that can no longer be mined and whose outputs nobody has spent through the acceptor. An acceptor SHOULD pay a fee the network accepts on the first attempt.
- **Availability.** Transfers wait while no acceptor key answers. Nothing is credited or lost while a request waits. Extra keys help only as far as accept-once allows: services that can each accept alone MUST share one consistent record of accepted outputs, or two of them could accept different spends of one output. Availability is therefore bounded by that record. A shared issuer set (§6) removes the shared record: `m` of `n` cosigners with `2m > n` keep separate records, and any `n − m` of them may be offline. The price is that its receipts are final once mined, not once countersigned.
- **Issuer exit.** An issuer that stops leaves transfers paused from its last mined beacon until the custodians release, at least `sil` seconds. A retiring issuer shortens the pause to however long the custodians take. Holders cannot exit alone while the asset is live, by design: a path that rescued holders without anyone's judgement would also release frozen ones.
- **Early release.** Custodians that release a live asset break the release rule, and end its freezes and limits early. The release is on chain, and the beacon chain alone disproves it: the beacon it spent had neither lapsed nor been retired. It frees frozen holders. It moves nobody's outputs.
- **Private release-branch spends.** Custodians can also countersign a live asset's transfer on the release branch without publishing a release, freeing one frozen holder in secret, outside accept-once. Script validation accepts such a spend. Resolution does not: a release-branch input needs a mined release (§11 step 6), so no conforming wallet credits it, and the issuer, which never accepted its outputs, refuses to countersign them later.
- **Freezes end at release.** A release ends every freeze, limit and registration rule, because the asset becomes owner-only. That is intended: the asset's value came from the issuer, so nothing is left for those rules to protect.
- **Finality after release.** A holder of a released asset can countersign two spends of one output. Recipients wait for mining (§9), as with any owner-only output.
- **Composability.** Closure fixes the script, so a holder cannot put the token in another contract, a marketplace covenant or a multisig without the acceptor countersigning that use.
- **Fees and spam.** The acceptor pays every network fee. Without fee terms or rate limits, small transfers cost the acceptor money.
- **Key compromise.** A stolen issuer key can accept transfers that break the issuer's policy, or accept an output twice. It cannot spend without the holder. With `m ≥ 2`, one stolen key is not enough. An issuer that loses control of its keys retires while it still can. Stealing `k` release keys lets the thief release the asset early, which frees frozen holders but moves no output. That is why release keys are held apart from the issuer's, under their own threshold.
- **Freeze, not seizure.** The acceptor freezes by refusing. It cannot move a frozen output. It can mint replacement supply when its authority permits, leaving the frozen output unspendable for good.
- **Replay.** Accept-once is per output. A request id identifies the exact transaction the holder signed, so a repeated request is the same request.
- **Holder signature scope.** `ANYONECANPAY` lets anyone add inputs to a holder's signed transaction. The outputs stay fixed, and no spend is valid without an acceptor, which adds only its own fee input.
- **Privacy.** The issuer sees every transfer and the identity behind each output, which is deliberate for a regulated asset. Nobody else does: every holder key is a fresh child, so receipts are not linked on chain, and a specific linkage (§5) reveals one output's owner to the acceptor it was encrypted to alone.
- **Remittance is custody.** A derived holder key is found again only from its remittance strings and the sender's identity key. A wallet that loses them cannot spend the output until it recovers them, from its history backup or from the sender's retained copy, so both sides keep them (§12).
- **Reorganization.** A reorganization can drop a transfer from the chain. The acceptor still holds and rebroadcasts the transaction it accepted, and cannot accept another spend of those inputs.

## Rationale

**Why the acceptance is in the script.** If acceptance were a separate signed statement, a transaction could reach the chain without it, and every later holder would need the statement for every hop. With the clause, a transaction without acceptance is invalid, and the recipient checks one transaction.

**Why Extended Format, not BEEF.** A countersignature commits to the script and amount it spends. So a recipient that is given those two things can check them. Nothing older than the transfer is needed. A one-input, two-output transfer with a 1-of-2 issuer set and a 2-of-3 release set is about 1 KB.

**Why release, not succession.** Nothing in a script can tell an issuer that is gone from one that is refusing you. A successor quorum that keeps accepting after the issuer would need trustees who judge, policy state handed over, and takeover statements carried to every wallet. But an asset this document is meant for takes its value from its issuer, so once the issuer is gone that control protects nothing. What holders still need is to move what they hold. A release gives them that in one on-chain transaction that every wallet finds by following the beacon, and the custodians make no judgement: they act on a retirement or a lapse that anyone can read from the chain.

**Why disclose keys.** The clause is fixed in every output already held. Publishing the release private keys turns the release branch into the holder's signature alone, without moving or rewriting any output.

**Why a beacon, not an endpoint.** Liveness has to be visible to the custodians and to holders without trusting the issuer's word, and without a server to ask. A mined beacon refresh is that signal: public, timestamped by the chain, and readable from any chain source.

**Why the issuer funds the transaction.** If the sender supplied a satoshi input, it could double spend that input and void an accepted transfer, leaving the token inputs stuck behind the accept-once rule. With the issuer's input as the only other input, acceptance is final the moment it is signed. A trade must carry the counterparty's satoshis, so it is sender-funded instead, and its recipients wait for mining (§6).

**Why an item is a supply of one.** An item needs what a token needs: an issuer that can refuse a transfer, offline resolution, and a way out when the issuer is gone. A deploy of supply 1 gets all of it with no new script and no ordinal tracking. Resolution's per-asset sum keeps it unique, and the deploy body fixes its content. Each kind still gets its own profile, `csft` or `csnft`, beside `p2pft` and `1sat`, so an application grant for one never reaches the other.

**Why membership spends the beacon.** After a release, wallets must still know which deploys were real, and a copied key set looks real on chain. Spending the collection's beacon needs `m` issuer keys, so the beacon chain is the member list, and minting also shows the issuer is alive.

**Why no offline payment.** Acceptance before resolution means the issuer must be reachable when a transfer is made. A live channel makes that one round trip, typically under a second. Only the sender and the issuer need the network; the recipient may be offline, because nothing it does can change the outcome once the issuer signed. An asset that must move with no network belongs under [BRC-175](../payments/0175.md), which accepts counterparty risk to do it.

**Compared with an overlay.** An overlay applies issuer policy by admitting transactions after they are built, and then has to say what an unadmitted output means. Here a policy refusal leaves nothing behind, and there is no admitted-but-maybe-spent state to attest.

## Open questions

1. **Key rotation.** Both key sets are fixed in every output. Rotating the issuer's keys means a migration countersigned into a new asset while it is live.
2. **Swaps.** Assets of one issuer already swap atomically: both holders sign one transaction with `0xC1`, and the acceptor countersigns each input (§11; the test script proves an item-for-tokens swap). Swaps against satoshis are sender-funded trades (§6). Two cases stay open:
   - **Across issuers.** Each acceptor must countersign the same transaction, and the first to sign has already marked its inputs spent. That needs a conditional accept, released when the other acceptor refuses or does not answer in time.
   - **Discovery.** How a holder finds a counterparty is outside this document. A swap offer is just a request whose outputs both sides already signed.
3. **Pre-accepted spends.** The issuer could sign `SINGLE | ANYONECANPAY` for one input and its paired output ahead of time, giving a voucher a holder can spend once to a fixed recipient while the issuer is away.

## Test script

[`media/0253/countersign.mjs`](./media/0253/countersign.mjs) builds a transfer with `@bsv/sdk`'s interpreter, with a 1-of-2 issuer set and a 2-of-3 release set. The holder signs, then the issuer adds its fee input and countersigns. The script checks that the input verifies from the Extended Format transaction alone, and that two release keys can countersign through their branch.

It also checks that each of these fails:

- a false spent amount, satoshi value, issuer set or release set;
- a holder signature with no countersignature;
- a countersignature by a key outside the sets;
- any change to outputs or inputs after countersigning;
- one release key alone;
- either group using the other's branch;
- release keys without the holder.

It then applies §11 offline with a small resolver: a token transfer and an item transfer resolve to the recipient, an item written twice is refused, a deploy paid straight to the recipient is refused, and an item-for-tokens swap under one issuer resolves the item to the buyer and the tokens to the seller.

It checks a §6 sender-funded sale: the seller signs its item input `0xC3` against a plain payment before any buyer exists, a buyer appends its item output and its own coin, the issuer countersigns, the input verifies and resolves the item to the buyer. Two buyers can each complete the same signed sale, and both spend the item; lowering the payment after the seller signed fails.

It checks the §6 replacement: the acceptor swaps its fee input, the holder's original signature still verifies, the txid changes and the outputs do not, and a replacement that changes an output fails. It shows that custodians can countersign a live asset's transfer on the release branch and pass script validation, that resolution refuses it while no release is known, and that it resolves once the release is.

It checks §2 and §5 holder keys: the recipient derives the private key of the BRC-29 child its sender wrote, which is not its identity hash; the acceptor opens the specific linkage sealed to it and finds `identityKey + linkage·G` hashes to the holder pubkey hash, while another acceptor key cannot open it; and the derived key spends under the issuer's countersignature.

Finally it checks §9: the issuer refreshes its beacon on the issuer branch, neither group can use the other's beacon branch, two release keys spend the final beacon and disclose their private keys, a disclosure of too few keys or a foreign key is no release, and after a release anyone countersigns with the disclosed keys while a stranger still cannot spend without the holder.

Run `npm install && npm test` in that directory.

It still accepts and resolves outputs locked to an identity key's pubkey hash or held under the `p color` keys of its earlier versions. The application spend label (§13) is refused in favour of a wallet method that runs §4 and §5.
